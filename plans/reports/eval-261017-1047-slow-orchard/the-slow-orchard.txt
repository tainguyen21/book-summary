The Slow Orchard
How the Town of Merrow Bay Planted a Public Orchard and Learned to Keep It Alive

A short book by the Merrow Bay Orchard Circle

Introduction

This book tells the story of a public orchard in Merrow Bay, a fishing town of about 6,200 people on a windy northern coast. Between the spring of 2019 and the autumn of 2024, a group of volunteers called the Orchard Circle planted 140 fruit trees on four pieces of unused public land. Some of those trees died. Most did not. By the final harvest described here, the orchard gave away 3.1 tonnes of fruit in a single season.

We wrote this book for other towns that want to try the same thing. It is not a gardening manual. It is an account of the decisions we made, the mistakes we paid for, and the three rules we ended up living by. Where we were wrong, we say so. Where we are still not sure, we say that too.

The short version is this: planting trees is easy, and keeping them alive is the whole job. Every chapter below comes back to that idea in one way or another.

Chapter One: Why an Orchard

The idea began at a town meeting in January 2019, when a retired ferry engineer named Odile Fenn asked why the strip of grass behind the old bus depot was mowed eleven times a year and used by nobody. The council's answer was that it had always been mowed. Fenn proposed planting it with fruit trees that anyone could pick from. The council agreed to a one-year trial on that single site, on the condition that the volunteers, not the council, would carry all the costs.

Fenn recruited nine people in the first month. They called themselves the Orchard Circle because, as Fenn put it, a committee sounds like it needs a chairman and a circle does not. The group had no money, no land of its own, and only one member, a schoolteacher named Ravi Osei, who had ever grafted a tree.

The group set out three goals. First, every tree would be free to pick, with no fences and no sign-up. Second, the orchard would use only varieties that could survive the coast's salt wind. Third, the orchard would cost the town nothing after the first five years. The first two goals were met. The third was only partly met, as Chapter Six explains.

Chapter Two: The First Planting and the Frost

In April 2019 the Circle planted 38 trees on the bus depot site: apples, pears, and plums bought as bare-root stock from a nursery two hundred kilometres to the south. The trees were cheap and looked healthy. Within six weeks, a late frost in the second week of May killed 17 of them, almost half of the first planting.

The group's first reaction was to blame the weather. Osei disagreed. He pointed out that the nursery trees had been grown in a mild inland valley and had already broken bud when they arrived, so they were weeks ahead of the coast's real spring. The frost did not kill healthy local trees; it killed trees that had been raised for a different climate. The lesson the Circle drew was not "plant later" but "plant trees that already know this place."

From the second year onward, the Circle stopped buying nursery stock. Instead, Osei taught volunteers to graft cuttings from old trees that had survived for decades in Merrow Bay gardens onto hardy rootstock. The grafted trees were smaller at first and took a year longer to fruit. In five years, the Circle lost only 9 grafted trees out of 102 planted, a loss rate of under nine percent, compared with nearly fifty percent in the first nursery planting.

A common belief among new volunteers was that frost cloth, the light fabric that gardeners drape over young plants, would have saved the 2019 trees. The Circle tested this in 2020 on a row of six leftover nursery trees and found that covered and uncovered trees suffered almost the same damage. The cloth was not the answer. Local stock was.

Chapter Three: Finding Land

After the trial year, the council allowed the Circle to plant on three more sites: a slope beside the primary school, the edge of the cemetery car park, and a triangle of land at the harbour roundabout. Each site taught something different.

The school slope had the best soil and the most helpers. Children watered the trees in exchange for being the first to pick them, which turned out to be the most reliable watering arrangement the Circle ever had.

The cemetery edge was the most sheltered site, and its pears did best there. Some townspeople objected at first that fruit trees beside graves were disrespectful. The Circle met with them twice and agreed to plant only blossom trees within ten metres of the oldest graves. The objections stopped, and several of the objectors later joined the pruning team.

The harbour roundabout was a failure. Of the 14 trees planted there in 2021, only 4 were alive in 2024. Road salt in winter and the constant spray from passing trucks were more than any variety could take. The Circle decided not to replant it and gave the land back to the council in 2023. We include it here because other towns will be tempted, as we were, by a visible site. Visibility is not worth much if the trees die.

Chapter Four: The Three Rules

By 2021 the Circle had argued its way to three rules, which it still follows.

The first rule is that no tree is planted unless a named person has promised to water it for its first two summers. The person's name goes on a small wooden tag on the stake. Trees with a named waterer survived at roughly twice the rate of trees planted in the first year, when watering was left to whoever happened to walk past.

The second rule is that pruning happens on one fixed Saturday in late February, called Cutting Day, and nobody prunes the public trees on any other day. Before this rule, enthusiastic volunteers pruned whenever they felt like it, and several young trees were cut back so hard that they did not fruit for two years. One fixed day meant one trained team, and the damage stopped.

The third rule is that fruit is never sold. The Circle was offered money twice, once by a cider maker and once by a supermarket, and refused both times. The reason was not purity. It was that a price would turn neighbours into customers, and customers complain instead of helping. Fruit that nobody picks is collected on the last Sunday of each month in season and given to the food bank and the school kitchen.

Chapter Five: The Harvest

The orchard's first real harvest came in 2022, when the grafted trees from 2020 began to fruit. That year the Circle weighed what it gave to the food bank and the school, and estimated what people picked for themselves, for a total of about 0.8 tonnes. In 2023 the total rose to about 1.9 tonnes. In 2024 it reached 3.1 tonnes.

The Circle is careful about these numbers. Only the food bank and school portions were actually weighed. The amount picked by passers-by was estimated by counting bare branches on a sample of trees, a method Osei admits is rough. The trend is real; the exact figures are not precise.

The most surprising result was not the weight of fruit but who picked it. In a small survey of 120 households in 2024, 41 percent said they had picked fruit from the orchard, and of those, more than half said they had never eaten a fruit they had picked themselves before. Several older residents said the orchard was the first new thing in the town in years that they had wanted to visit.

Chapter Six: Money

The Circle's third goal was that the orchard would cost the town nothing after five years. The truth is more mixed.

Over five years the Circle spent about 9,400 euros. The largest costs were rootstock and stakes, a shared water tank for the school slope, and insurance for volunteers. Most of the money came from a yearly plant sale of spare grafted saplings, which raised between 1,100 and 1,800 euros a year, and from small donations.

The council did end up paying for one thing: in 2023 it agreed to cover the volunteers' insurance, about 600 euros a year, after the Circle's own insurer raised its price. So the orchard is not free to the town, but it costs the town less than the mowing it replaced, which the council's own figures put at about 1,500 euros a year for the bus depot strip alone.

Chapter Seven: What We Would Tell Another Town

If we were starting again, we would do four things differently.

We would never buy trees raised in a different climate. Grafting from local survivors is slower but it is the single decision that saved the orchard.

We would refuse visible but hostile sites like the harbour roundabout, however much the council or the newspaper likes them.

We would put the named-waterer rule in place on the first day, not the third year.

And we would weigh everything, not just the portion we gave away, so that our numbers would be easier to trust.

We would not change the decision to give the fruit away. Of everything the Circle did, that was the choice that made the orchard belong to the town rather than to the volunteers.

Closing

Odile Fenn stepped down from the Circle in 2024 and handed the pruning shears to Ravi Osei's former pupils, three of whom now run Cutting Day. The orchard is not finished. Some of the 2020 trees will not be at full size for another decade. But the question that started it, why mow a field that nobody uses, has been answered. Now it is used.
